## The Big Picture

RLVR raises accuracy, but the headline number hides a cost: problems the model had already solved become
unsolvable again as training continues. The paper frames every RL update as driving two opposing flows:

- **Acquisition flow** ↑: problems the model newly solves.
- **Regression flow** ↓: problems it used to solve and no longer does.

Together they call this **correct-set turnover**, and it turns retention into an explicit optimization target
alongside acquisition. The goal is to minimize regression while preserving acquisition, which means answering two
questions:

1. **Which** mastered prompts are at risk?
2. **When** should we intervene?

Their answer is **ReMind**: a retention-aware review mechanism that tracks mastered prompts and periodically
reintroduces them to remind the model of previous solutions.

The authors call this forgetting *intra-task*, to separate it from the cross-task setting of classic continual
learning, where task A is forgotten while training on task B. Here there is one fixed training set and no task
switch. Under the broader definition of continual RL, though, this is still a continual learning problem: the
mechanism is interference through shared parameters, the same thing my Elephant notes analyze with
$\left<\nabla_w f_w(x), \nabla_w f_w(x_t)\right>$.


## Mastery and Correct-Set Turnover

Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the training set. For a prompt $p \in \mathcal{P}$, the true success probability of the
current policy is $q_t(p) = \Pr[\pi_{\theta_t} \text{ solves } p]$, estimated from $K$ rollouts:

\[
\hat q_t(p) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{1}[r_i = 1]
\]

A prompt is **mastered** at step $t$ if every rollout is correct, giving the mastered set

\[
M_t = \left\{p \in \mathcal{P} : \hat q_t(p) = 1\right\}
\]

Two properties of $\hat q_t(p)$ matter:

- **Property 1: it is graded.** With $K = 16$, a prompt at $12/16$ is already drifting from mastery, but a single
  evaluation would still call it correct 75% of the time. The binary right/wrong signal from prior
  example-forgetting work (Toneva et al., 2019) can't see this regime.
- **Property 2: it is free.** $\hat q_t(p)$ is the group's mean reward, which GRPO already computes for its
  advantages. No extra evaluation is needed.

A **regressed** prompt ("regress" = go backward) is one that was mastered at some step and later has
$\hat q < 1$. Missing even one rollout counts. With $K = 8$:

| Step | Rollouts correct | $\hat q$ | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 8/8 | 1.00 | Mastered |
| 80 | 6/8 | 0.75 | Regressed (drifting) |
| 100 | 0/8 | 0.00 | Fully forgotten |

One caveat I noted: the strict rule is noisy with few rollouts. A prompt the model solves 90% of the time scores
8/8 only $0.9^8 \approx 43\%$ of the time, so some measured "regression" is sampling luck rather than forgetting.


### Acquisition, regression and retention

$\Delta$ is just a gap in training steps. Comparing $M_t$ with $M_{t+\Delta}$ splits the net change into two
flows:

- **Acquisition:** $p \notin M_t$ and $p \in M_{t+\Delta}$.
- **Regression:** $p \in M_t$ and $p \notin M_{t+\Delta}$.

\[
\text{Retention}_t = \frac{|M_t \cap M_{t+\Delta}|}{|M_t|},
\qquad
\text{Regression}_t = 1 - \text{Retention}_t
\]

Both lie between 0 and 1, since the intersection can never be larger than $M_t$:

\[
0 \le \text{Retention}_t \le 1, \qquad 0 \le \text{Regression}_t \le 1
\]

Example: $M_t = \{A, B, C\}$ and $M_{t+\Delta} = \{B, C, D, E\}$. Acquisition is $\{D, E\}$, regression is
$\{A\}$, the net change is $+1$, and retention is $2/3$. The $+1$ hides one lost problem.

In the figures they also track **correct-set loss**: the *count* of problems ever solved that the current policy
fails. It is not a training loss, just "lost from the correct set". On a tracked set,

\[
\text{currently solved} = \text{ever solved} - \text{correct-set loss}
\]


### The plateau observation

Training Qwen3-VL-8B with GRPO, validation accuracy stalls after about 100 steps while correct-set loss keeps
climbing. The paper reads the plateau as new solutions being cancelled out by forgotten ones.

That is only part of the story. In Figure 5(b) acquisition itself slows down sharply after about step 150, so the
plateau is acquisition slowing *and* forgetting continuing. Correct-set loss can also rise mechanically, because
the pool of ever-solved problems keeps growing and lucky one-off solves count as later "forgetting".


## The Repair-Window Principle

The abstract claims to "analytically and empirically establish" this principle. My definitions of the pieces:

- **Cost of restoring:** how much training it takes to bring a regressed prompt back to full mastery.
- **Review delay:** how many steps pass between a prompt being mastered and it being trained on again.
- **Low-cost window:** the period right after mastery, while the prompt has drifted only slightly and repair is
  still cheap.
- **Pipelines fail to exploit it:** standard training keeps no record of which prompts are mastered. A prompt
  only returns when the data loader reaches it again, typically the next epoch, which is too long.


### Drift through gradient interference

Once a prompt is mastered and absent from the batch, updates from other prompts still move it. To first order:

\[
q_{t+1}(p) \approx q_t(p) - \alpha\,\nabla_\theta q_t(p)^\top g_t
\]

where $g_t$ is the batch gradient and $\alpha$ the learning rate. $\nabla_\theta q_t(p)^\top g_t$ captures
**gradient interference**. Writing $g_t$ as the batch average makes the link to the NTK explicit:

\[
\nabla_\theta q_t(p)^\top g_t = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j,i,t} A_{j,i}\,\nabla_\theta q_t(p)^\top \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta\left(y_{j,i,t} \mid x_j, y_{j,i,<t}\right)
\]

Each inner product is a kernel entry between $p$ and one batch token, the same
$K_w(x, x_t) = \left<\nabla_w f_w(x), \nabla_w f_w(x_t)\right>$ as in the Elephant paper. Elephant attacks
this through architecture (make the kernel sparse); ReMind attacks it through data scheduling (re-check before
the drift gets large).

With $q_{t_0}(p) \approx 1$, after $k$ unreviewed steps the paper models the success probability as

\[
q_{t_0 + k}(p) \approx 1 - k\alpha\delta(p)
\]

where $\delta(p) > 0$ is an unknown positive scalar summarizing the per-step interference magnitude for $p$.


### The $\alpha^2$ argument doesn't hold up

The paper then compares two costs:

\[
\text{early cost} \sim O(k\alpha\delta) \quad (\text{how much it drifted}),
\qquad
\text{late cost} \sim O(1/\alpha) \quad (\text{steps to relearn})
\]

\[
\frac{O(k\alpha\delta)}{O(1/\alpha)} = O(k\alpha\delta \cdot \alpha) = O(k\alpha^2\delta)
\]

and concludes that because this "ratio" is tiny for $\alpha \sim 10^{-6}$, early intervention is cheaper.

This is not clearly explained, and I don't think it means anything:

- **The units don't match.** The numerator is a drop in probability; the denominator is a number of steps. The
  paper never defines "cost", so there is no principled way to divide one by the other.
- **The sanity check fails.** At full forgetting, $k\alpha\delta \approx 1$, early and late repair are the same
  situation and the ratio should be about 1. The formula gives $(k\alpha\delta)\cdot\alpha \approx \alpha = 10^{-6}$.
  It is small for any $k$ just because $\alpha^2$ is small.
- **The drift model breaks before full forgetting.** For $k > 1/(\alpha\delta)$ it predicts $q < 0$. It is a
  first-order approximation, only valid near $q \approx 1$.

A consistent version measures both sides in steps. Drift is about $\alpha\delta$ per step and one review update
repairs about $\alpha c$, so

\[
\frac{\text{early repair steps}}{\text{late relearning steps}} \approx \frac{k\delta / c}{1/\alpha} = \frac{k\alpha\delta}{c}
\]

The ratio is on the order of the drift itself: slipping 5% costs about 5% of relearning, and full forgetting costs
about as much as relearning.

The paragraph also contradicts itself: it says early review gives a "self-reinforcing gradient that restores
$q$", and that since all rollouts are correct the review adds almost no gradient noise. If all rollouts are
correct, every GRPO advantage is zero, so there is no gradient at all, neither noise nor repair.


### What actually makes late repair expensive

GRPO only learns from a prompt when its $K$ rollouts are *mixed*. The chance of that is

\[
P(\text{mixed group}) = 1 - q^K - (1-q)^K
\]

| $q$ | $P(\text{mixed})$ with $K = 8$ | Situation |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 0% | Mastered: nothing to learn |
| 0.9 | 57% | Slightly drifted: repair is fast |
| 0.5 | 99% | Maximum signal |
| 0.01 | 8% | Mostly forgotten: must stumble onto a correct answer first |
| 0 | 0% | Fully forgotten: no gradient, back to learning from scratch |

The repair window is really the range where groups are still mixed. As $q \to 0$ the learning signal doesn't
shrink proportionally, it disappears. That is the real mechanism, and it needs no $\alpha^2$.


### The empirical check

From a step-60 GRPO checkpoint they take prompts that are 16/16 correct and compare:

- **Early review:** included immediately. Strict retention stays at 85–91% (peak 91.5%).
- **Delayed review:** withheld for 20 steps. Retention falls to 78–84%; after review resumes it recovers to a
  peak of 87.7% but never closes the gap (at most 14 fewer prompts retained).

This is the real evidence, and it is narrower than the claim: one delay length against none, at one checkpoint.
"Cost grows sharply with delay" would need a sweep over delays.


## ReMind

ReMind adds one mechanism to GRPO: a review queue that reintroduces mastered prompts into the batch *before*
rollout generation. It has three parts.

**1. Review queue.** When a prompt reaches $\hat q_t(p) = 1$ and isn't already queued, it is appended to a FIFO
queue with probability $p_{\text{add}} = 0.25$. Only prompt IDs are stored, so every review generates fresh
rollouts from the current policy. FIFO order means the prompts mastered longest ago are checked first.

**2. Batch construction.** Every $f$ steps is a review step.

- Non-review steps: the batch is drawn entirely from the training sampler.
- Review steps: a fraction $r$ of the batch comes from the queue $Q_t$.

\[
\widetilde{B}_t = B_t^{\text{fresh}} \cup B_t^{\text{review}},
\qquad
|B_t^{\text{review}}| = \min\left(\lfloor r \cdot |\widetilde{B}_t| \rfloor,\ |Q_t|\right)
\]

This is **pre-rollout batch replacement**: review prompts *replace* fresh ones before generation, so the rollout
count doesn't change. With a batch of 256, $K = 8$, $r = 0.10$ and $f = 5$:

| Step | Fresh prompts | Review prompts | Rollouts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 256 | 0 | 2,048 |
| Review (every 5th) | 231 | 25 | 2,048 |

That works out to $r/f = 2\%$ of training prompts. It isn't free: displaced fresh prompts get pushed later, and
review steps still add about 3% wall-clock time.

**3. Queue update.** After rewards are computed:

- A newly mastered prompt is enqueued with probability $p_{\text{add}}$.
- A review prompt that is still fully correct is **released** (removed from the queue).
- A review prompt that regressed ($\hat q_t(p) < 1$) goes back to the **tail** for another check.

So the queue gradually fills with the prompts that keep slipping. Two life cycles:

- Stable: mastered at step 40 → enqueued → reviewed at 55, still 8/8 → released.
- Unstable: mastered at 40 → reviewed at 55, 5/8 → trains on the mixed group, requeued → reviewed at 80, 8/8 →
  released.

Two things to keep in mind:

- **The "reminding" only happens for slipped prompts.** A review prompt that is still 8/8 has zero advantage and
  teaches the model nothing; it only confirms retention. Under DAPO it would simply be filtered out.
- **"Retain" has two meanings.** A prompt that *retains* mastery leaves the queue; a *regressed* prompt is
  *retained* in the queue.


### Blind spots

- **Released prompts are never checked again.** One successful check releases a prompt for good. The main runs
  are about one epoch (450 steps × 256 prompts ≈ the 123K training set), so a released prompt almost never comes
  back through the normal stream. If it regresses later, nothing notices.
- **75% of mastered prompts are never enqueued at all.** With $p_{\text{add}} = 0.25$ most mastered prompts are
  unprotected from the start.
- **One check is weak evidence of stability,** because 8/8 is noisy and a prompt stable at step 55 can still
  drift later.

The paper cites Ebbinghaus and spaced repetition, but real spaced repetition (e.g. Leitner boxes) doesn't retire
an item after one success; it *lengthens the interval*. Requeueing with a doubling delay, or requiring several
consecutive passes before release, would be closer to the idea.


## Replay vs. ReMind

The baselines ExGRPO, RLEP and RePO are replay methods. The difference is what they store:

| | Replay | ReMind |
|---|---|---|
| Stores | Old correct responses | Prompt IDs only |
| Trains on | Responses from an older policy (off-policy) | Fresh rollouts from the current policy |
| Knows if a prompt was forgotten? | No, the stored answer is correct by construction | Yes, via $\hat q_t(p)$ |
| Gradient on review | Always | Only if the prompt slipped |
| Batch | Usually augmented | Replaced |

None of the three replays mastered prompts specifically; they target efficiency. ExGRPO even does the opposite of
ReMind: it moves prompts solved in every rollout to a "retired set" and stops replaying them. RLEP replays
verified-correct solutions from an earlier run, and RePO keeps a per-prompt buffer of past outputs to fix
zero-gradient groups.

Replay does have one advantage: at 0/8 ReMind gets no gradient, but a stored correct solution can still pull a
fully forgotten prompt back. A hybrid (re-test with fresh rollouts, fall back to a stored solution at 0/8) seems
worth trying.


## Results

Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct trained on MMFineReason-123K, evaluated on 8 image-text benchmarks (Table 1):

| Method | MathVision | WeMath | Avg (8 benchmarks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base model | 47.37 | 54.10 | 57.88 |
| RePO (best replay) | 53.77 | 63.52 | 62.02 |
| GRPO | 48.82 | 56.57 | 60.25 |
| GRPO + ReMind | 56.81 | 66.38 | 64.06 |
| DAPO | 52.78 | 57.43 | 61.02 |
| DAPO + ReMind | 57.32 | 68.29 | 64.46 |

The headline deltas (+7.99 on MathVision, +9.81 on WeMath) are measured against plain GRPO, which barely improves
on the base model on those two benchmarks. Against the best replay baseline the gains are about +3 on each and
**+2.0 on average**. Still a solid result for about 2% of batch slots, if it survives multiple seeds.

It also generalizes: video understanding goes from 62.88 to 66.09 over GRPO, and on text math with
Qwen2.5-7B-Math ReMind reaches 52.61 vs. 51.38 for ExGRPO. Gains are larger on math than general benchmarks
(+5.67 vs. +1.94 over GRPO), which the paper attributes to longer reasoning chains being more fragile.

Things to be careful about:

- **One run per method,** no seeds or variance.
- **The budget ablation suggests about ±1 point of noise.** 1%, 2%, 4% and 10% review budgets score 62.98,
  64.06, 63.19 and 62.83, with no clear trend, and the default happens to be best.
- **A small claim that doesn't match the table.** The text says all three replay methods score below GRPO on
  ZeroBench, but RePO's 23.50 is above GRPO's 22.60; only ExGRPO (19.01) and RLEP (19.61) are below.
- **Checkpoint selection** (final vs. best) isn't stated.


### Two epochs vs. ReMind

The obvious objection is that ReMind just shows the model data again, so a second epoch should do the same.
Two-epoch GRPO oscillates around 0.50, peaks at 0.524 at step 740 and ends at 0.497. ReMind reaches 0.524 at
step 296 (2.5× faster) and ends at 0.539.

The "+4.1 pp" compares ReMind's end with the two-epoch run's end, near the bottom of an oscillation; against the
two-epoch peak it is about 1.5 points. As an efficiency result it is convincing (double the compute vs. about 3%
more), but "overfitting" is asserted rather than shown, and only one alternative was tried.


### Where the retention gains land

Stratifying 700 validation prompts by base-model accuracy: on unsolvable prompts (0%, $n = 240$) GRPO and ReMind
are the same, since review can only protect something already learned. On fragile prompts (0–50%, $n = 292$)
ReMind is +5.1 pp better, consolidates 46.2% of them above 80% accuracy (vs. 40.8%), and fully forgets 6.8%
(vs. 11.0%).


## Training Dynamics and Entropy

Figure 5 is the mechanism claim: both methods acquire new solutions at the same rate (about 500 ever solved by
step 450), but GRPO ends with about 190 forgotten prompts vs. about 155 for ReMind, and accuracy is 0.47 vs. 0.52.
Across 79 review events, per-event retention rises from 72% to 82%, yet 4–5 prompts still regress at every event.

The surprising part is entropy. It isn't targeted at all, but ReMind's entropy rises from about 0.4 to about 0.8
while GRPO's falls to about 0.2. The paper links the two: under GRPO, entropy decline and forgetting onset both
accelerate between steps 50 and 150, and review "counteracts this concentration".

Reading the plots against the entropy mechanism paper, several things don't fit:

- **Early forgetting is identical despite opposite entropy trends.** In panel (c) ReMind's forgotten count rises
  just as fast over steps 50–150, while its entropy is rising. The methods only separate after about step 200.
- **The accuracy gap appears before the forgetting gap.** Around step 100 ReMind already leads (about 0.44 vs.
  0.41) while acquisition and forgetting are nearly identical, so the early lead isn't explained by retention.
- **The covariance picture predicts the opposite.** Only slipped review prompts give gradients, and those are
  mostly-correct groups. Their correct rollouts are *confident and right* and their wrong rollouts tend to
  contain *unlikely and wrong* tokens, and both cells lower entropy. Review should sharpen, not flatten, unless the
  regressed prompts drifted to a confident wrong answer.
- **Higher isn't automatically better.** The entropy paper found no link between entropy level and performance,
  and ReMind's entropy is still climbing at step 450 rather than leveling off.

So the entropy result is striking, but it is reported as an observation with a plausible story, not a mechanism.


## Takeaways and Open Questions

- **Mitigation, not a solution.** The authors say so themselves: ReMind reduces but doesn't eliminate regression,
  and about 155 prompts are still forgotten. That's roughly an 18% reduction.
- **The plateau has two causes.** Acquisition slows down and forgetting continues; ReMind recovers a few points by
  reducing the second. The entropy paper's story (collapse limits exploration) would explain the first.
- **Pass@k is motivation only.** The introduction cites RLVR narrowing pass@k, but every result is accuracy.
  ReMind protects prompts mastered *during training*, not problems the base model solved once in 256 tries, so it
  shouldn't be read as a pass@k fix.
- **The repair-window math is a heuristic.** $O(k\alpha^2\delta)$ adds a look of rigor without adding
  information; Figure 2 is the real evidence.
- **Missing baselines:** ExGRPO without its retired set, re-testing *all* solved prompts without a queue, and
  spaced-repetition intervals instead of one-shot release.
