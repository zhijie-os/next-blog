## Background: LLMs Are Softmax Policies

An LLM is a softmax policy over its vocabulary. The state $s$ is the prompt plus every token generated so far,
and the action $a$ is the next token.

\[
\pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp(z_{s,a})}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} \exp(z_{s,a'})}
\]

Here $z_{s,a}$ is the output logit of action $a$ given state $s$, with $s \sim d_{\pi_\theta}$ and
$a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot \mid s)$. Two facts I kept using:

- Only differences between logits matter. Adding the same constant to every logit leaves $\pi_\theta$ unchanged.
- $\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$, which makes the softmax derivative simple:

\[
\frac{\partial \pi(b)}{\partial z_a} = \pi(b)\left(\mathbb{1}\{a=b\} - \pi(a)\right)
\]


### Where the logits come from

A transformer layer is attention plus an MLP, each wrapped in RMSNorm and a residual connection:

\[
\begin{aligned}
x &\leftarrow x + \mathrm{Attention}(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)) \\
x &\leftarrow x + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x))
\end{aligned}
\]

Attention mixes information across positions; the MLP transforms each position on its own. After the last layer
and a final RMSNorm, the LM head turns the hidden state into logits:

\[
z = Wh, \qquad z_a = w_a^\top h
\]

- $W$ has one row per token, shape `vocab_size × hidden_size`. It starts random and is learned during pretraining.
- $h$ is the final hidden state at a position. All the context lives here; $W$ is the same at every position.

The input embedding *encodes* (token IDs → vectors) and the LM head *decodes* (final hidden state → one logit per
vocabulary token). In full-parameter RL, which is what this paper does, the LM head is still trainable; under LoRA
it is usually frozen. Either way its structure comes from pretraining, and RL's small learning rate adjusts it
rather than reorganizing it.


### Tabular vs. LLM softmax

The theory in the paper assumes a *tabular* softmax policy, where every state-action pair has its own parameter:

\[
z_{s,a} = \theta_{s,a}
\]

An LLM is not tabular. Its logits come from shared weights, $z_{s,a} = w_a^\top h_\theta(s)$, so one gradient step
moves logits at many states at once. This matters later: Lemma 1 only needs a softmax, so it holds for LLMs as long
as we plug in the actual logit change. Proposition 1 and Theorems 1-2 compute that logit change assuming
tabularity, so for LLMs they are approximations.


## The Predictable Collapse of Entropy

Without entropy or KL regularization, entropy drops sharply at the start of RL and keeps falling toward zero, while
validation accuracy rises and then plateaus. Across 11 runs, 73% of the entropy drop and 76% of the performance
gain happen in the first 200 of 2,400 steps.

Accuracy $R$ and entropy $\mathcal{H}$ fit

\[
R = -a\exp(\mathcal{H}) + b
\]

so the ceiling at $\mathcal{H} = 0$ is $R = -a + b$. (The takeaway box in Section 2 writes
$-a\exp(\mathcal{H} + b)$; Eq. 6 is the correct form.) Fitting on only the first 36 steps predicts the next 200
with an RMSE of about 0.9% on math and 1.2% on code. The coefficients look independent of the algorithm (GRPO,
RLOO, PRIME and REINFORCE++ collapse onto one curve) and scale log-linearly with model size within Qwen2.5.

One caveat: entropy and accuracy are both smooth, monotone functions of the training step, so a smooth monotone fit
between them is partly expected. The early-prediction result is the more convincing part.


## Entropy as Average Surprise

\[
\mathcal{H}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\left[-\log \pi(a)\right] = -\sum_a \pi(a)\log\pi(a)
\]

- $\log\pi(a) \le 0$ is the log-likelihood of token $a$.
- $-\log\pi(a)$ is the surprise of token $a$.
- Entropy is the *average* surprise, weighted by $\pi$. Surprise belongs to one token; entropy belongs to the whole
  distribution at a position.

The log comes from additivity. Surprise should add for independent events, $I(pq) = I(p) + I(q)$, and $-\log p$ is
the only continuous function that does this while being zero at $p = 1$ and decreasing in $p$. The same property
lets sequence entropy split into per-token terms, which is why averaging token-level entropy makes sense.

Example with $\pi = [0.7,\ 0.2,\ 0.1]$:

\[
\mathcal{H} = -\left(0.7\log 0.7 + 0.2\log 0.2 + 0.1\log 0.1\right) \approx 0.80 \text{ nats}
\]

$\exp(\mathcal{H})$ is the effective number of choices: 1 nat is roughly 3 live options per token, 0.1 nat is
roughly one.


## Lemma 1: Entropy Change Is a Covariance

\[
\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta^{k+1}) - \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta^{k})
\approx
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_{\pi_\theta}}\left[
-\mathrm{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot \mid s)}\left(
\log \pi_\theta^k(a \mid s),\;
z^{k+1}_{s,a} - z^{k}_{s,a}
\right)\right]
\]

- $\log\pi_\theta^k(a \mid s)$ is the log-prob *before* the update.
- $z^{k+1}_{s,a} - z^{k}_{s,a}$ is how much the logit moved.

The covariance is taken over the tokens at one state, weighted by $\pi$. It asks: are the tokens that are already
likely the ones whose logits go up?


### Derivation

I wanted every step. Work at one state and write $\Delta z_a = z^{k+1}_{s,a} - z^{k}_{s,a}$.

**Step 1: first-order Taylor expansion.** Entropy is a function of all the logits, so

\[
\mathcal{H}(z + \Delta z) \approx \mathcal{H}(z) + \sum_a \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial z_a}\Delta z_a
\]

and with $\Delta\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}(z + \Delta z) - \mathcal{H}(z)$,

\[
\Delta\mathcal{H} \approx \sum_a \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial z_a}\Delta z_a
\]

**Step 2: derivative of softmax (quotient rule).** With $Z = \sum_c e^{z_c}$ and
$\partial Z / \partial z_a = e^{z_a}$:

\[
\frac{\partial \pi(a)}{\partial z_a} = \frac{e^{z_a}Z - e^{z_a}e^{z_a}}{Z^2} = \pi(a)\left(1 - \pi(a)\right),
\qquad
\frac{\partial \pi(b)}{\partial z_a} = -\frac{e^{z_b}e^{z_a}}{Z^2} = -\pi(b)\pi(a) \quad (b \neq a)
\]

Both cases together:

\[
\frac{\partial \pi(b)}{\partial z_a} = \pi(b)\left(\mathbb{1}\{a=b\} - \pi(a)\right)
\]

**Step 3: derivative of entropy with respect to one probability (product rule).**

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial \pi(b)} = -\left(\log\pi(b) + 1\right)
\]

**Step 4: chain rule.** Changing $z_a$ changes every $\pi(b)$, so sum over $b$:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial z_a}
= \sum_b \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial \pi(b)}\frac{\partial \pi(b)}{\partial z_a}
= -\sum_b \left(\log\pi(b) + 1\right)\pi(b)\left(\mathbb{1}\{a=b\} - \pi(a)\right)
\]

**Step 5: split the sum.** The indicator keeps only $b = a$ in the first part, and $\pi(a)$ factors out of the
second:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial z_a}
&= -\pi(a)\left(\log\pi(a) + 1\right) + \pi(a)\sum_b \pi(b)\left(\log\pi(b) + 1\right) \\
&= -\pi(a)\left(\log\pi(a) + 1\right) + \pi(a)\left(\overline{\log\pi} + 1\right) \\
&= -\pi(a)\left(\log\pi(a) - \overline{\log\pi}\right)
\end{aligned}
\]

where $\overline{\log\pi} = \sum_b \pi(b)\log\pi(b) = -\mathcal{H}$. The $+1$'s cancel because
$\sum_b \pi(b) = 1$.

**Step 6: plug into Step 1.**

\[
\Delta\mathcal{H}
\approx -\sum_a \pi(a)\left(\log\pi(a) - \overline{\log\pi}\right)\Delta z_a
= -\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\left[\left(\log\pi(a) - \overline{\log\pi}\right)\Delta z_a\right]
\]

**Step 7: recognize the covariance.** With $X = \log\pi$ and $Y = \Delta z$,

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[(X - \bar X)(Y - \bar Y)\right]
= \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \bar X)Y\right] - \bar Y\,\mathbb{E}\left[X - \bar X\right]
= \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \bar X)Y\right]
\]

since $\mathbb{E}[X - \bar X] = \mathbb{E}[X] - \bar X = 0$: the $\pi$-weighted deviations from the $\pi$-weighted
mean cancel. So centering $\Delta z$ is optional, and

\[
\Delta\mathcal{H} \approx -\mathrm{Cov}_{a \sim \pi}\left(\log\pi(a),\ \Delta z_a\right)
\]

Nobody negates anything by choice; the minus falls out of Step 5. Likely tokens have a negative entropy gradient,
so boosting them lowers entropy.

**Numeric check.** $\pi = [0.7,\ 0.2,\ 0.1]$ and $\Delta z = [1,\ 0,\ 0]$. The centered log-probs are
$[+0.45,\ -0.81,\ -1.50]$, so

\[
\mathrm{Cov} \approx 0.7 \times 0.45 \times 1 = 0.31, \qquad \Delta\mathcal{H} \approx -0.31
\]

The exact change is $0.80 \to 0.48$, or $-0.32$.


### Reading the covariance

Covariance measures whether two quantities move together:

- $\mathrm{Cov} > 0$: above-average $X$ goes with above-average $Y$, or below with below.
- $\mathrm{Cov} < 0$: they move in opposite directions.
- $\mathrm{Cov} = 0$: no linear relationship.

So a positive covariance of $(\log\pi, \Delta z)$ means entropy goes down, and a negative one means entropy goes up.

"High probability" means $\log\pi$ is above its $\pi$-weighted average, i.e. $\log\pi(a) > -\mathcal{H}$: the token
is less surprising than average. It does not mean $\log\pi > 0$, which never happens. The zero point doesn't matter
anyway, since $\mathrm{Cov}(X + c, Y) = \mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)$.

| | Logit goes up | Logit goes down |
|---|---|---|
| **High-probability token** ($X - \bar X > 0$) | entropy ↓ | entropy ↑ |
| **Low-probability token** ($X - \bar X < 0$) | entropy ↑ | entropy ↓ |

Only *relative* logit changes matter: $\Delta z = [+1,\ +1,\ +1]$ gives zero covariance and no entropy change. And
$\Delta\mathcal{H}$ is a rate, not a level. A small positive covariance still means entropy keeps falling, just
slowly.


## Theorems 1 & 2: Plugging in the Update Rule

Lemma 1 says nothing about *how* the logits change. That comes from the training algorithm.

**Advantage.** $A(s,a) = Q(s,a) - V(s)$ is how much better action $a$ is than the policy's average from $s$. It
averages to zero under the policy, $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[A(s,a)] = 0$ (Lemma 3 in the appendix). GRPO estimates
it per response, and every token in the response shares it:

\[
A = \frac{r(y) - \mathrm{mean}(r)}{\mathrm{std}(r)}
\]

With 3 of 8 responses correct, correct responses get about $+1.29$ and wrong ones about $-0.77$.

**Vanilla policy gradient (Proposition 1).** For a tabular softmax,

\[
z^{k+1}_{s,a} - z^{k}_{s,a} = \eta\,\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\,A(s,a)
\]

so (Theorem 1)

\[
\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta^{k+1} \mid s) - \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta^{k} \mid s)
\approx -\eta\,\mathrm{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot \mid s)}\left(\log\pi_\theta^k(a \mid s),\ \pi_\theta^k(a \mid s)\,A(s,a)\right)
\]

**Natural policy gradient.** NPG is steepest ascent where step size is measured by KL between policies instead of
Euclidean distance in parameter space, $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta F^{-1}\nabla J$ with Fisher matrix $F$. It is
still policy gradient; the KL metric changes the direction, not just the length. For a tabular softmax the logit
change is just $\eta A(s,a)$, so (Theorem 2)

\[
\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta^{k+1} \mid s) - \mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta^{k} \mid s)
\approx -\eta\,\mathrm{Cov}_{a \sim \pi_\theta^k(\cdot \mid s)}\left(\log\pi_\theta^k(a \mid s),\ A(s,a)\right)
\]

Both theorems give the same sign table:

| | High advantage ($A > 0$) | Low advantage ($A < 0$) |
|---|---|---|
| **High probability** | entropy ↓ (confident and right) | entropy ↑ (confident and wrong) |
| **Low probability** | entropy ↑ (unlikely but right) | entropy ↓ (unlikely and wrong) |

The difference is weighting. Under vanilla PG a rare token's logit moves by $\eta\pi A$, so "unlikely but right" is
damped by its small probability. Under NPG every logit moves by $\eta A$.

The paper's argument for why the covariance is positive in practice: a pretrained model is reasonably calibrated,
so its confident outputs tend to be correct. Probability and advantage line up, and RL keeps sharpening.


## Empirical Verification (Section 3.3)

The theory predicts

\[
-\Delta\mathcal{H} \propto \mathrm{Cov}(\cdot)
\]

They run on-policy GRPO without the PPO surrogate on Qwen2.5-7B, treating each response as one action with a
length-normalized log-prob. In Figure 8 (left), the covariance and the entropy decrease share a shape: large and
positive early, then smaller but still positive, which explains why entropy only falls. In Figure 8 (right), easy
prompts have higher covariance than hard ones.

This supports the direction of the effect, but it is not a quantitative check. The curves are on separate axes,
the constant can't literally be $\eta = 5\times10^{-7}$, and the covariance is sequence-level while entropy is
token-level.


## Why Standard Entropy Control Fails (Section 4.1)

Normally in RL we encourage exploration with an entropy bonus, or penalize KL divergence from a reference model.
Neither works well here.

**Entropy bonus.** `entropy_coef` is $\alpha$ in

\[
L_{\text{ent}} = L - \alpha\,\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta)
\]

This $L$ is a loss (lower is better), so subtracting $\alpha\mathcal{H}$ rewards entropy. In Figure 9, 0.0001 and
0.001 barely change entropy, 0.01 makes it explode past 5, and 0.005 stabilizes it without beating the baseline.
Adding entropy loss does not boost performance. The bonus flattens every token, including the many positions where
the model should be certain.

**Reference KL.** `kl_coef` is $\beta$ in

\[
L_{\text{KL}} = L + \beta\,D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}),
\qquad
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\left[\log\pi_\theta(a) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a)\right]
\]

In Figure 10, a larger $\beta$ keeps entropy stable but degrades performance. This felt counter-intuitive until I
split the KL:

\[
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathcal{H}(\pi) + \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\left[-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a)\right]
\]

It contains an entropy bonus, but also a pull toward the reference model's favorite tokens. The KL-regularized
optimum is

\[
\pi^*(a) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(a)\exp\left(r(a)/\beta\right)
\]

so a larger $\beta$ holds the *whole policy* near the base model, which is exactly what RL is trying to move away
from. The reference KL is mainly useful in RLHF, where a learned reward model can be hacked. With a verifiable reward
there is little to protect against, so it mostly costs performance.


## Clip-Cov

Section 4 restricts the update of high-covariance tokens instead of regularizing every token. Each token gets a
centered cross-product (Eq. 10):

\[
\mathrm{Cov}(y_i) = \left(\log\pi_\theta(y_i) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\pi_\theta(y_j)\right)\cdot\left(A(y_i) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A(y_j)\right)
\]

This is each token's term in Theorem 2's covariance. $N$ is the number of tokens scored together; in the verl
implementation that is the set of response tokens passed to one loss call (a micro-batch), not the full rollout
batch.

The distribution is extremely heavy-tailed (Table 1, Qwen2.5-7B, step 1):

| Tokens | Mean covariance |
|---|---|
| Top 0.02% | 5.654 |
| Top 0.2% | 3.112 |
| Top 2% | 1.385 |
| All | 0.003 |

The top 0.2% alone contribute about twice the whole batch's covariance, so the other 99.8% are net negative. A
handful of tokens drive the collapse.

Clip-Cov clips a small fraction of high-covariance tokens out of the policy gradient update:

\[
I_{\text{clip}} = I \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\left(\left\{i \mid \mathrm{Cov}(y_i) \in [\omega_{\text{low}}, \omega_{\text{high}}]\right\},\ \lfloor r \cdot N \rfloor\right)
\]

This is a random subset of the outliers, of size $\lfloor r \cdot N \rfloor$, where $r$ is the clip ratio. The paper
uses $\omega_{\text{low}} = 1$, $\omega_{\text{high}} = 5$ and $r = 2\times10^{-4}$. A new subset is drawn each
update, and tokens scoring above 5 are never touched.

\[
L_{\text{Clip-Cov}}(\theta) =
\begin{cases}
\mathbb{E}_t\left[\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid y_{<t})}A_t\right], & t \notin I_{\text{clip}} \\[2ex]
0, & t \in I_{\text{clip}}
\end{cases}
\]

Two notes to self:

- This $L$ is an **objective** to maximize (ratio × advantage, like PPO's $L^{\text{CLIP}}$), even though Section 4.1
  uses $L$ for a loss. The code minimizes $-A_t \rho_t$. If you minimized Eq. 12 as written, a positive advantage
  would push $\pi_\theta$ *below* $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
- Setting a term to 0 just means the token contributes no gradient: Clip-Cov **skips the gradient** for these tokens.
  Their probabilities can still move through shared parameters. The implementation also keeps PPO's ratio clipping
  for everything else.

Written with a consistent sign convention, and with the PPO clipping the code keeps:

\[
J_{\text{Clip-Cov}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\mathbb{1}\{t \notin I_{\text{clip}}\}\cdot\min\left(\rho_t A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)A_t\right)\right],
\qquad
L_{\text{Clip-Cov}} = -J_{\text{Clip-Cov}}
\]

with importance ratio $\rho_t = \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid y_{<t})$.


## KL-Cov

Rank the tokens by covariance and take the top $k$ fraction:

\[
I_{\text{KL}} = \left\{i \mid \mathrm{Rank}(\mathrm{Cov}(y_i)) \le k \cdot N\right\}
\]

$\mathrm{Rank}$ sorts tokens by score, highest first. $k$ is the proportion of tokens subjected to the KL penalty,
with $k \ll 1$: $2\times10^{-3}$ for 7B and $2\times10^{-4}$ for 32B. Unlike Clip-Cov, there is no upper bound and no
randomness.

\[
L_{\text{KL-Cov}}(\theta) =
\begin{cases}
\mathbb{E}_t\left[\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid y_{<t})}A_t\right], & t \notin I_{\text{KL}} \\[2ex]
\mathbb{E}_t\left[\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid y_{<t})}A_t - \beta\,D_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid y_{<t}) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})\right)\right], & t \in I_{\text{KL}}
\end{cases}
\]

with $\beta = 1$.

- Notation: $y_t$ is the $t$-th response token and $y_{<t} = (y_1, \dots, y_{t-1})$ is everything before it (the
  prompt $x$ is conditioned on too, just dropped). So $\pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t})$ is one number: the probability
  of the sampled token given its prefix.
- That also means the KL as written compares two numbers, which isn't defined. The intended KL is over the full
  distributions $\pi(\cdot \mid y_{<t})$, and the code uses a single-sample stand-in,
  $\left\lvert \log\pi_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}) - \log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid y_{<t}) \right\rvert$.
- The anchor is the rollout policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, not the initial model, so this only slows each round
  of updates. With $\beta = 1$, the absolute-value penalty acts like a dead zone: selected tokens with
  $\lvert A_t \rvert \lesssim 1$ are held in place, and larger ones move more slowly.
- In the pseudo-code, KL-Cov replaces PPO's ratio clipping rather than adding to it.

Both selections in a few lines (simplified from Listing 1; `log_prob` and `adv` are 1-D tensors over the response
tokens, padding ignored):

```python
cov = (log_prob - log_prob.mean()) * (adv - adv.mean())   # Eq. 10
n = int(ratio * cov.numel())

# Clip-Cov: a random subset of tokens with lb < cov < ub gets no gradient
cand = torch.nonzero((cov > lb) & (cov < ub)).squeeze(-1)
clip_idx = cand[torch.randperm(cand.numel())[:n]]

# KL-Cov: the top-n tokens by cov get a penalty toward the rollout policy
kl_idx = torch.topk(cov, n).indices
```

Both work as entropy dials (Figure 12): clipping more tokens or raising $\beta$ gives higher entropy, and KL-Cov
gives smoother curves.


## Results

Qwen2.5-7B and 32B, trained on DAPO-MATH (256 prompts × 8 responses, 8 policy updates per rollout batch) and
evaluated on 7 math benchmarks. Averages from Table 2:

| Model | GRPO | Clip-higher | Clip-Cov | KL-Cov |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen2.5-7B | 38.6 | 38.8 | 40.4 | 40.6 |
| Qwen2.5-32B | 45.8 | 47.2 | 50.3 | 52.2 |

KL-Cov has a slight edge, mostly on 32B, and gives smoother entropy curves. It was also tuned per model size ($k$
differs) while Clip-Cov wasn't, and each configuration is a single run.

The headline 15.0% and 14.6% are KL-Cov vs. GRPO on AIME24 and AIME25 for the 32B model:

| | GRPO | KL-Cov | Gain | Out of 30 problems |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AIME24 | 21.8 | 36.8 | +15.0 points | ≈ 4.5 more |
| AIME25 | 16.2 | 30.8 | +14.6 points | ≈ 4.4 more |

These are percentage points (avg@32), not relative gains. Against Clip-higher the AIME24 edge is only about 0.4
problems. On easier benchmarks the gain is minimal: MATH-500 goes from 84.2 to 84.6 for KL-Cov on 32B (about 2 of
500 problems), while Clip-higher drops to 77.2 on the same benchmark, a reminder of how noisy single runs are.


## Takeaways and Open Questions

- **Entropy looks like a symptom, not a target.** The entropy bonus raised entropy with no gain, and the authors
  report no relationship between the entropy level their methods maintain and final performance (Section 4.5).
- **The selected tokens are probably low-probability ones.** Since $\log\pi \le 0$, a confident token can sit at most
  about $\mathcal{H}$ above the average log-prob, while a rare token can sit several nats below. With GRPO advantages
  capped near $\pm 2.65$, confident tokens top out around $\mathcal{H} \times 2.65$, roughly 1 or less, so scores of
  3 to 5 have to come from rare tokens in wrong responses (the "unlikely and wrong" cell). The paper doesn't report
  what the selected tokens look like, so this is arithmetic, not measurement.
- **Missing controls.** Restraining the same number of random tokens, or tuning the entropy bonus to match KL-Cov's
  entropy, would show whether selecting by covariance is what matters.
- **The theory is tabular.** Lemma 1 holds for any softmax, but the logit changes in Theorems 1-2 assume
  independent logits, and the empirical check is qualitative.
